Product overview

About SelahCue — what it is and how it works

SelahCue is a desktop application that runs the visual side of a church service — everything the congregation, the stage team and an online stream see on screen — with a companion phone/tablet app to control it, and optional AI assistance that helps but never takes over.

What it is

A cross-platform application for Windows, macOS and Linux, with a mobile controller for Android and iPhone/iPad. It is built for churches, conferences, worship events, livestream productions and sermon recording. One person — the media operator — drives the whole visual service from a single desktop, and the software keeps every screen showing exactly the right thing at the right moment.

The core idea: one live service, many screens

A service isn't shown on a single screen — it appears on several at once, and each needs something different at the same instant. SelahCue drives them all from one source of truth. At one moment in a single song, for example:

ScreenWhat it shows at that moment
Main auditorium projectorFour lines of lyrics over a moving background
Stage / confidence monitorThe current line, the next line, a clock and the service timer — for the worship leader
Livestream outputTwo lines of lyrics as a lower-third caption for the online audience
Lobby displayAnnouncements, continuing independently

How a service runs, step by step

  1. Before the service, a coordinator builds a service plan — the ordered run-sheet of songs, scriptures, media and announcements — and SelahCue checks that every media file is present and ready.
  2. During the service, the operator stages the next item in a preview, then sends it live with a click or keypress. What is being prepared is never accidentally shown to the congregation.
  3. Scripture is found by reference or keyword and placed on screen in the chosen translation and layout.
  4. Timers keep segments on schedule, with a configurable "TIME UP" signal that can be shown discreetly to the stage without ever appearing on the audience screen.
  5. Emergencies — an instant blackout or a screen clear — are always one action away, and work with no network at all.

Control from a phone or tablet

Team members can control the service from a mobile device over the church's local Wi-Fi — advancing lyrics from the stage, running timers from the floor, or approving scripture from a tablet. Pairing is by QR code, connections are encrypted, and each person gets only the controls their role allows. Crucially, the desktop is always in charge: if a phone loses connection, the live service is completely unaffected.

Optional, responsible AI assistance

When enabled (in a later release), SelahCue can listen to the sermon and transcribe it, suggest the scripture the preacher is referencing for the operator to approve, and afterwards draft editable sermon notes. This assistance is always operator-in-the-loop — the software proposes, a human confirms — so an AI mistake can never reach the screen on its own.

The promise that underpins everything
SelahCue is built offline-first and reliability-first. The core of a service — slides, scripture, media, timers, blackout — keeps working with no internet, no cloud and no phone connected, and no failure of any single part (a lost screen, a failed AI service, a dropped network) can ever blank or clear what the congregation is seeing. It also saves continuously and restores its exact live state after a crash.

Section 2

2 · How we work — and why it's built this way

SelahCue will run during live church services, conferences and livestreams — moments where software failure is highly visible and cannot be undone. That reality shapes our entire method. We optimise for correctness and reliability, not speed of typing.

Stage gates with human approval

Work advances through numbered stages. At the end of each, we stop, present evidence, and wait for your explicit decision to continue. Approval authorises only the next stage — never a blank cheque for the whole project.

Why
It keeps you in control of scope, cost and direction at every step, and guarantees that expensive work (like writing code) never begins on an unapproved foundation.

Goal Contracts & measurable completion

Every stage and task runs against a written "Goal Contract" with Boolean, verifiable pass/fail criteria. Work is only "done" when those criteria pass with evidence — never because someone believes it looks finished. A small validation program checks each contract's structure automatically.

Why
"It looks correct" is how defects reach production. Objective, checkable criteria remove wishful thinking from the definition of done.

Evidence, classified for honesty

Every research finding is tagged as Observed (we saw or measured it), Documented (a credible source states it), Inferred (reasoned, not proven), or Unknown (needs investigation). We deliberately record what we don't yet know.

Why
It prevents marketing claims and guesses from masquerading as facts. When we say a number is unproven, you know exactly how much to trust it.

Independent verification

High-stakes claims — the requirements audit, security, quality — are checked by fresh reviewers who did not do the original work. No one signs off on their own homework.

Why
Authors are blind to their own gaps. An independent adversarial reviewer catches what the writer cannot see — as proven in Stage 4, where 17 real issues were found and fixed.

One source of truth for delivery

Delivery work lives in your ClickUp workspace (the BUILD CONTROL — SelahCue task and its dedicated list). Durable product knowledge — this report, the PRD, research, decisions — lives in version-controlled documents. Progress is always recoverable without relying on chat history.

Section 5

5 · Stage 2 — Discovery & research

What we did. Six specialist researchers worked in parallel — competitor analysis, AI/transcription capabilities, licensing & compliance, cross-platform feasibility, security & privacy, and user roles & workflows — producing evidence-classified findings. A panel of four independent reviewers then audited the whole package (verdict: sound, with conditions we resolved). Where the brief demanded it, we did a follow-up study of dedicated transcription products.

The findings that shaped the product

Being honest about AI

This is where church-AI products most often over-promise. Our research is deliberately candid:

  • Live transcription works well for clear, close-microphone speech but is materially less accurate in a real room (echo, PA bleed, accents, biblical names). Critically, the underlying technology can invent plausible-sounding text during silence or music — a serious risk in worship.
  • Automatic scripture detection is reliable when the preacher plainly states a reference ("John 3:16"), fallible for exact quotations, and unreliable for paraphrase. We refuse to claim a single accuracy figure we cannot prove.
Design consequence
AI is an operator assistant, not an autopilot. By default, a human confirms every AI suggestion before it appears on screen, the deterministic parser is trusted over guesswork, and the system is tuned to avoid false positives. Every AI error is recoverable before the congregation ever sees it.

Licensing is a hard boundary

We can freely bundle only public-domain Bible translations (e.g. the World English Bible, ASV, Berean). Copyrighted translations (NIV, ESV, etc.) and copyrighted song lyrics may not be bundled — they remain the church's own licensed responsibility, which SelahCue supports rather than supplies. We also mapped video-codec patent and data-protection (NDPA/GDPR) obligations.

A technology reality check

The brief suggested a specific technology stack. Our feasibility work confirmed most of it is sound but surfaced one important correction — the lightweight app framework cannot double as the high-performance screen "compositor," which needs a dedicated graphics engine. We captured ten precise technical questions ("spikes") to answer before committing the architecture in Stage 5, rather than guessing now.

Reliability as a promise, not a feature

The non-negotiable rule. The desktop application is always in charge. Core presentation — slides, local scripture, media, blackout, timers — must keep working with zero network, internet, mobile or AI availability, and no failure of any component may blank or clear what's on screen as a side effect. A dropped phone connection, a failed AI provider, or an unplugged screen must never take down the service.

An early security threat-model mapped the biggest risk — the local-network link between phone and desktop on shared church Wi-Fi — and the protections needed (encrypted, paired, permission-checked, replay-protected connections). We also modelled 11 user roles and a 7-role mobile permission matrix.

Section 6

6 · A key decision — removing Text-to-Speech

The original brief included a Text-to-Speech (TTS) feature — the app reading text aloud. Partway through, you decided to hold off on it: "I don't see it as a major requirement in the long run."

Decision DEC-001 — TTS removed from the roadmap
TTS is reclassified from a "later release" feature to an explicit non-goal, revisited only if you request it in future. This is fully recorded and traceable.

Why this was the right call

Our own research had already rated TTS the lowest-value of the four AI features (a congregation reads the screen; it rarely needs the app to speak) and the highest-risk in a live room — audio feedback loops, microphone bleed, and the danger of speech accidentally playing through the main sound system. Removing it reduces cost and risk and simplifies the audio design, with no downside to the core product. The accessibility needs it might have served are met through other means (keyboard operation, high-contrast large text, screen-reader support).

Nothing was lost: the TTS research remains on file, so the decision is fully reversible if priorities change.

Why every requirement is measurable
A vague requirement ("the app should be fast") cannot be tested, so it cannot be trusted. Each SelahCue requirement states a number or an observable behaviour — for example, "a slide appears on the main screen within 150 milliseconds of the operator's command." That is something quality engineers can objectively verify later.

What the PRD covers

Service planning · slide and lyric editing · the full Bible scripture system · multiple independent screen outputs · stage and confidence displays · lower-third graphics · timers and a configurable "TIME UP" state · media playback · autosave and crash recovery · emergency clear and blackout · mobile discovery, pairing and secure control · live transcription · automatic scripture detection · AI sermon notes · production integrations · plus complete sections on permissions, data lifecycle, offline behaviour, security, privacy, accessibility, reliability, performance and licensing.

8 · Stage 4 — The independent audit

What we did. A panel of five fresh reviewers — none of whom wrote the PRD — audited it against a strict checklist: missing workflows, untestable criteria, unrealistic AI expectations, privacy/licensing/security/accessibility gaps, and over-sized scope. A lead reviewer then adjudicated a single verdict.

Audit roundVerdictBlocking issuesMajor issues
First auditPass with conditions017

Why this is the most valuable stage in the report
The audit did its job: it found 17 genuine issues the authors had missed — and it found them on paper, where a fix costs minutes, instead of in shipped code, where it could cost weeks or cause a live-service failure. This is exactly the return the disciplined method is designed to produce.

Examples of what the audit caught — and how we fixed it

The gap the auditors foundHow we resolved it
What happens if the disk fills up during a long service? Autosave could silently fail.Added a requirement for early low-disk warnings and protected space reserved for crash recovery.
If a crash is caused by the very content being restored, the app could crash-loop endlessly.Added a "crash-loop breaker": after repeated crashes the app offers to start clean and disables the suspected item.
Removing TTS was clean in the main document but stale references lingered in supporting files.Completed the removal everywhere, so no one builds a permission or workflow for a cancelled feature.
Even without TTS, the app's own media/alert audio could be re-recorded by the transcription microphone and trigger false detections.Added a guard that pauses transcription while the app plays audio into the room.
Encrypted connections were specified for control commands but not for the live transcript sent to phones.Broadened encryption to all data sent to mobile devices, including previews and transcripts.
Flashing "TIME UP" graphics posed a photosensitive-seizure risk on public screens.Added a seizure-safety limit (no more than three flashes per second) and a reduced-motion option.

All 17 fixes were re-verified by fresh reviewers, confirming a clean PASS with no new problems introduced. The PRD is now cleared for the architecture and design stage.

Section 9

9 · The first release (MVP) — what ships first, and why

The complete vision is large. Trying to build all of it at once is the single biggest risk to the project, so we defined a focused, genuinely useful first release and phased the rest.

In the first release

MVP The reliable presentation core on Windows, macOS and Linux, plus a secure mobile controller: service plans; slide, lyric and scripture presentation (public-domain Bibles); a main audience screen and a stage/confidence display; lower-third graphics; timers with "TIME UP"; media playback; autosave, crash recovery, emergency clear and blackout; mobile pairing with encrypted, permission-checked control of slides and timers; plus the security, privacy, accessibility and reliability foundations.

Deliberately held for later releases

ReleaseAddsWhy later
R2Extra screens, transparent lower-thirds, broadcast (NDI) output, diagnosticsValuable but not essential to a working service; depends on performance proven by the Stage-5 spikes.
R3Live transcription & transcript exportNeeds the reliable core in place first; carries the most AI/privacy complexity.
R4Automatic scripture detectionBuilds directly on transcription (R3).
R5AI sermon notesBuilds on the transcript; post-service, not live-critical.
R6Production integrations, macros, right-to-left languagesAdvanced/ecosystem features for power users.

Explicitly excluded

Non-goal Text-to-Speech (removed, DEC-001) · voice cloning · bundling any copyrighted Bible translation or song lyrics · copying any competitor's code or interface.

Why bound the MVP so tightly
A smaller, excellent, reliable first release that churches can trust on a Sunday morning is worth far more than a sprawling, fragile one. Each later release adds value on a proven foundation.
